# Humor and Voice: A Working Guide

How the writing on The Burgundy Wire gets funny, and how it gets it wrong. Built from the posts themselves rather than general comedy advice, because the goal is to sound more like you, not more like a comedian.

Every example below is a real line from a published post. The ones marked ★ are lines you specifically called out as working.

---

## The short version

1. **The engine is the gap between hope and reality.** That's your phrase, and it's the right one. Every Commanders fan has lived in that gap for decades, so writing from inside it makes readers feel seen.
2. **Kind, with a backbone of cynicism.** Also yours. The cynicism is the seasoning, not the meal. Posts land warm.
3. **Your signature move is the wrong vocabulary.** You describe football and life using the language of some completely unrelated field: medicine, law, bureaucracy, engineering. It's the most reliable joke in your writing, and it's yours.
4. **Specific beats general, every time.** Not "an old TV," but "a television so deep it should've had its own zip code."
5. **The funny word goes last.** Structure the sentence so the payoff is the final thing the reader hits.
6. **Undersell the emotional parts.** Your best sentimental writing is the quietest.
7. **Never build a joke on a fact you haven't checked.** A wrong fact doesn't just kill the joke, it makes the reader distrust the rest of the post.

---

## 0. The premise comes first

Points 1 and 2 above are the ones you set yourself, early on, and they sit above everything else here because they decide what a post is *about*. The techniques in the rest of this document are how a sentence gets funny. These decide whether the whole post is.

**The gap between hope and reality.** The funniest material this season came from the distance between what you expected and what happened: predicting 10-7 in August, then watching "injuries stacking up like it's a hobby"; being sure you'd snuck a win by Week 2, then "Reader, it was not the one." Hope is the setup, reality is the punchline, and three decades of Commanders fandom means the reader already knows the joke's shape before you tell it. That shared history is why the humor works here and wouldn't for a neutral audience.

**Kind, with a backbone of cynicism.** Your words: *"us commanders fans have a darkness inside us, but i want to make sure its always positive and happy."* The darkness is real and earned, so it shows up. But it's seasoning. Every post ends with some version of hope, even if the hope is "well, at least there's PBR."

**A few structural rules you've already settled on:**

- **Lead with the single dumbest or most self-incriminating detail.** It buys trust immediately. The CRT essay opens on being too young to consent to fandom; the 4:38 post opens on being awake at 4:38 on purpose.
- **One loop per post.** Open a thread early, close it late. One, not three, or the ending turns into a list of callbacks.
- **End on the Commanders.** Always. A post that's 80% about a concert still lands on burgundy.
- **The best paragraphs contrast the two halves of your life** (the catered President's Box and a franchise on fire) rather than describe either one.
- **When your memory is provably wrong, don't quietly fix it.** Write the unreliability in as the joke, and settle the real facts with you separately. The FSU post kept a fused memory of two different games on purpose. This is the one exception to "check the fact first": you can print a wrong memory, as long as the post *knows* it's a memory and says so.

## 1. The signature move: the wrong vocabulary

The technical name is a *register shift*: you take a situation and describe it in the formal vocabulary of some unrelated domain. The humor comes from the mismatch between the dignified language and the undignified subject. It's all over the CRT essay, and it's the thing that makes your writing sound like you.

Sorted by the field you're borrowing from:

**Medicine and diagnosis**
> when my fandom stopped being a hobby and became a **personality disorder**

> Splitting my attention across this many teams in this many states is not a skill, **it is a condition.**

> which is a sentence that should come with a **surgeon general's warning**

**Bureaucracy and paperwork**
> she still married me anyway. That's either love or a **filing error.** ★

> The reigning champs can **file a complaint.**

**Law and crime**
> Any one of them could have quietly murdered me in my sleep and **no jury in the country could've pieced together the motive.**

> Wire Taps Is Open, and My Weekend Is a **Logistical Crime Scene**

**Engineering and technology**
> This team was installed in me, like **factory firmware**, before I was old enough to consent to anything.

> the **load-bearing event** of the entire weekend

**Retail and ordering**
> which is fine for a rookie and still not **the August I ordered.**

**Romance and breakups**
> So **it is not you, it is the calendar**, and I will make it up in wings.

**Why it works:** a hobby becoming a "personality disorder" is a real observation about obsession, but saying it in clinical language lets you make it without sounding like you're complaining. The formality does the work of irony, so you never have to wink.

**How to use it:** when you've got a plain sentence that states a fact about fandom, ask what professional would describe this situation, and borrow their words. "I'm obsessed" becomes a diagnosis. "I was wrong" becomes a filing error. "It was a disaster" becomes a crime scene.

**The one caution:** it's so reliable that it can become a tic. Two or three per post is seasoning. Seven is a formula, and readers feel the formula before they can name it.

## 2. The mechanics that make a line land

### Put the funny word last

The payoff should be the last thing in the sentence. Readers pause at the end of a sentence, so that pause should be where the laugh is.

> That's either love or a **filing error.**

> He was right and the rest of us were **cowards.**

> I was him for about **four hours.**

Compare the version with the payoff buried: *"It was a filing error or love, one of the two."* Same words, no joke. When a line isn't landing, the first thing to try is moving the funny word to the end.

### Add the tag

A tag is a second, smaller joke after the first one has landed. It rewards the reader for laughing and usually gets a bigger laugh than the setup.

> If you are old enough to remember Cornholio, I was that guy. **I was him for about four hours.**

> I understand how that sounds. **I have accepted how that sounds.**

The first sentence is the joke. The second sentence is the tag. The Cornholio line without its tag is fine. With it, it's the line people quote.

### Specific beats general

Every good image in the CRT essay is concrete.

> a television so deep it should've had its own zip code

> watching men in waistcoats assault a banjo

"An old TV" and "a folk band" are information. "Its own zip code" and "assault a banjo" are pictures. If you catch yourself writing an adjective like *old*, *big*, *crazy*, or *intense*, that's usually a spot where a specific image would be funnier.

### Short sentences for timing

Rhythm is most of comic timing on the page. Short declaratives force the reader to slow down.

> There was a Redskins-Cowboys game. There was a flood. There was a ditch.

Three flat sentences, and the reader assembles the disaster themselves. It's funnier than describing it, because you withheld the connecting tissue and let them do the work.

### Diminishing qualifiers

Start with a claim, then shrink it, one step at a time.

> I have grown since then. **Some. A little.** Enough to know better and still not always do better.

The shrinking is the joke. And notice where it lands: on something true. The best version of this move ends with an honest line, not another joke.

### The list of three that breaks on the third

The classic structure: two items set a pattern, the third breaks it.

> Whether that's **love, Stockholm syndrome, or just twenty-plus years of proximity to my nonsense**

Love is sincere. Stockholm syndrome escalates. The third one deflates, and the deflation is self-deprecating, which makes it warm rather than mean.

### Comment on your own sentence

Stepping outside the prose to react to what you just wrote.

> landed walking distance from bars in my hometown, **which is a sentence that should come with a surgeon general's warning**

It works because it's honest about how the sentence reads. Use sparingly. Once per post is plenty.

## 3. Building a bit across a post, and across posts

### The callback

A joke planted early and paid off late. The CRT essay ends by pulling its whole arc back together:

> Wood paneling to Slack, a ditch to a proposal, one dad's old CRT to whatever this website turns into.

Every item in that list was set up earlier. The ending feels earned because the reader recognizes all of it.

### The receipt

This is the rarest and best version, and it happened this season. The 4:38 A.M. post said, in print, before the game:

> by pure mojo ... we are going to take this game. I have decided. The reigning champs can file a complaint.

Then the Monday post opened:

> I told you so. ... Final score: Washington 33, Seattle 31. **File your complaint.**

That's a callback across two separate posts, with a receipt the reader can go and check. Most "I told you so" writing is a bluff. This one had evidence, which is why it's funny instead of smug. **Worth doing on purpose:** a confident, specific prediction in a pregame post is a setup you can pay off Monday, whichever way it goes. If you're wrong, the Monday post is a mea culpa, which is also funny.

### Running bits

Bits that recur across posts build a relationship with regular readers. They get funnier each time because the reader is in on it. See the list at the bottom of this document.

## 4. The emotional writing

Your best sentimental writing is understated, and the reason is structural, not stylistic.

> My brother Nathan passed away years ago now, and he was with us on Sunday the way he is at all of these. **Nobody had to say it out loud.** ★

You described that paragraph as "understated in the best possible way," and that's exactly the mechanism. It states the fact plainly, says only what's actually true, and then **describes the silence instead of filling it.** Readers bring their own grief to it. Adding more (what he'd have thought of the game, what he meant to you) would have given them less room.

Three rules that fall out of it:

1. **Say only what you know.** The Nathan paragraph works partly because it doesn't reach for anything it can't support.
2. **Let the reader connect it.** That post ends by saying the scoreboard was "only part of why" this was your favorite game. It never says *because of Nathan*. The reader gets there alone, and it lands harder for it.
3. **Put the emotion where the ending can call back to it.** An emotional beat in the middle gives your closing line somewhere to point.

And one about placement: a joke immediately after a sincere moment reads as flinching. Give the heavy beat a paragraph of its own, then come back to the jokes.

## 5. Who the joke is on

**Mostly on you.** Self-deprecation is the backbone of the voice, and it's why the voice is likable. You're the guy who ended up in a ditch, who manufactured legal cocaine out of Red Bull, who helps out while Nicole does the actual work.

**Up, not down.** The Cowboys, the champs, and a punter the whole league is somehow emotionally invested in are all fair game. People having a bad day are not.

**Damning without being mean.** The Seahawks fan section is the model. It worked because it was *specific*, not because it was harsh:

> The guy directly in front of me applauded when a Commander went down injured. **Applauded.**

No insult, no adjective. It reports what happened, repeats the one word that matters, and moves on. The reader supplies the anger. It also explicitly allowed that plenty of Seattle fans were normal, which is exactly what let it be fair while still being blunt. Describe the behavior precisely and the judgment writes itself.

**Real people get checked first.** Friends, family, and anyone named in a post are real, and they'll read it. Before a joke lands on a named person, it's worth asking whether they'd laugh too.

## 6. What hasn't worked

These come from your own corrections this season. They're the most useful part of this document.

**Leaning on one device.** An early draft of the Dallas pregame built the whole post out of subreddit quotes. You asked for "bits and pieces so it looks cohesive" instead, and it was a much better post. Any single device, Reddit quotes included, stops being funny once the reader can predict it.

**A joke built on a wrong fact.** This happened four times in one post: you'd never been to Philadelphia, the car-window guy wasn't yelling at you, UVA did play that weekend, and Bernthal was bad information. None of those were jokes on their own, but each one had a joke resting on it. A wrong fact under a joke is the worst outcome available: the reader stops laughing *and* stops trusting the post. Check first, write second.

**The wrong register for the room.** "Car park," "honour," "grey." One Britishism and a first-person paragraph about tailgating in Landover sounds like someone else wrote it. American English, spelling and vocabulary both.

**Framing someone the way they don't want to be framed.** Nicole doesn't want to be cast as the event planner, so a near-miss became "four grown adults, four phones, four clocks, one departure time." Collective blame turned out to be funnier anyway.

**Leaning too hard on an obvious comparison.** You asked not to push the RG3 parallel too far, because Jayden isn't that guy and the injury isn't that injury. The post named the comparison once and deliberately pulled back from it. The restraint was more affecting than the comparison would have been.

**Explaining the joke.** If a line needs a follow-up sentence to explain why it's funny, cut the explanation. If it still doesn't work, the line was the problem.

**Looking designed.** Not a writing note, but the same instinct: you called a bordered-card callout "vibe codish." Anything that looks like it came from a template undercuts a voice that's supposed to sound like a person.

## 7. Titles

The titles that have worked do one of three things:

**Borrow a lyric or phrase and bend it.**
> Friends in Low Places, Enemies in Dallas ★

> A Case of the Mondays: Much Too Young to Feel This Damn Old

Both take a Garth Brooks title and make it about the weekend. The first one bends the original; the second just lands it straight because it happened to be exactly true.

**Deliver the payoff.**
> A Case of the Mondays: I Told You So

Short on purpose, because the post has the receipt.

**Promise specific, mismatched things.**
> Crab Cakes, a Censored Head Coach, and the Week Everyone's Cold Finally Left the Building

A list of concrete, unrelated items is its own small joke, and it tells the reader this is going to be personal rather than analytical.

What to avoid: titles that summarize ("Week 3 Recap: Commanders Beat Seahawks"). That's what every other site writes, and it's the opposite of the voice.

## 9. Before you publish

- [ ] **Is every fact under a joke actually true?** Scores, names, who was where, who yelled at whom.
- [ ] **Have you already told this story?** Check the running bits above and past posts before teasing something as new. A callback to an old post is great; promising it as a future one isn't.
- [ ] **Does each joke end on its funniest word?** If not, try reordering the sentence.
- [ ] **Is there at least one specific image** where a general adjective would have been easier?
- [ ] **Are you leaning on one device too hard?** Count the Reddit quotes, the register shifts, the lists of three.
- [ ] **Is the heavy moment given room**, with no joke right on top of it?
- [ ] **Would every named person laugh at their own line?**
- [ ] **Does the ending call back to something earlier?**
- [ ] **American spelling and vocabulary.** Parking lot, honor, gray, favorite.
- [ ] **No em dashes.** Anywhere.
